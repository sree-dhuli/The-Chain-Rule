01 · The Rule

What is the Chain Rule?

Every differentiation rule you've learned so far (Power Rule, Product Rule, Quotient Rule) handles functions built from basic pieces. But what about a function like \(\sin(x^2)\) or \((3x+1)^{10}\)? These are composite functions: one function plugged inside another. The Chain Rule is the tool that handles them.

The Chain Rule
\[\frac{d}{dx}\!\left[f(g(x))\right] = f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)\]
Differentiate the outside (leaving the inside alone), then multiply by the derivative of the inside.

In plain English: derivative of the outside × derivative of the inside. The inside function is never touched when you differentiate the outside.

🧅 The Onion Analogy

Think of a composite function as an onion. The Chain Rule says: peel one layer at a time, and multiply the results together. Each layer you peel gives you one factor in your answer. The key is recognizing how many layers exist before you start.

Step 1: Identify the layers

Before differentiating, ask yourself: "what is the outermost operation, and what is plugged into it?" Name them \(f\) (outer) and \(g\) (inner).

Outer function \(f\)
Apply to the whole inside
∘
Inner function \(g\)
What's plugged in

For example, in \(\sin(x^2)\): the outer function is \(\sin(\square)\) and the inner function is \(x^2\). In \((3x+1)^{10}\): the outer is \((\square)^{10}\) and the inner is \(3x+1\).

Step 2: Apply the formula

Differentiate outer

Take the derivative of \(f\), but keep \(g(x)\) plugged in exactly as-is.

\(\sin(x^2) \to \cos(x^2)\), and the \(x^2\) stays.
Multiply by inner's derivative

Multiply by \(g'(x)\), the derivative of what was inside.

\(\cos(x^2) \cdot \dfrac{d}{dx}[x^2] = \cos(x^2)\cdot 2x\)

Leibniz notation form

If you write \(y = f(u)\) where \(u = g(x)\), the Chain Rule looks like:

\[\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}\]
The \(du\)'s "cancel" (informally), which is a useful memory device

Both forms say the same thing. The Leibniz form makes it visually clear why you multiply: the rates chain together.

02 · Why It Works

Deriving the Chain Rule

The Chain Rule follows from the limit definition of the derivative. Here is the intuitive derivation using the Leibniz form.

Proof sketch via limits
1.We want \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) where \(y=f(u)\) and \(u=g(x)\). By the limit definition: \[\frac{dy}{dx}=\lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\]
2.Assuming \(\Delta u\neq 0\), multiply and divide by \(\Delta u\): \[\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta u}\cdot\frac{\Delta u}{\Delta x}\]
3.Take the limit as \(\Delta x\to 0\). Since \(g\) is differentiable (hence continuous), \(\Delta u\to 0\) as well: \[\lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta u}=\frac{dy}{du}=f'(u)\]
4.And simultaneously: \[\lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{\Delta u}{\Delta x}=\frac{du}{dx}=g'(x)\]
5.Combining: \[\boxed{\frac{dy}{dx}=f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)}\]

Note: the full rigorous proof requires handling the edge case where \(\Delta u = 0\) in a neighborhood around the point, which is done cleanly via a small reformulation. The above captures the essential logic.

Why the inside is left unchanged

When you differentiate \(f\) (the outer function), you're computing the rate at which the output of \(f\) changes per unit change in its input. And its input is \(g(x)\), not \(x\). So \(g(x)\) stays inside \(f'\) exactly as-is. Then multiplying by \(g'(x)\) converts "per unit \(g\)" into "per unit \(x\)".

Example 01 Differentiate \(y=(3x^2+1)^5\)
1
Identify the layers.
Outer: \(f(u)=u^5\).   Inner: \(g(x)=3x^2+1\).
2
Differentiate the outer, leaving the inner untouched.
\(f'(u)=5u^4 \implies 5(3x^2+1)^4\)
The Power Rule applies to the outer, so the \(3x^2+1\) stays inside.
3
Differentiate the inner function.
\(g'(x)=\dfrac{d}{dx}[3x^2+1]=6x\)
4
Multiply: outer derivative × inner derivative.
\(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=5(3x^2+1)^4\cdot 6x=30x(3x^2+1)^4\)
Example 02 Differentiate \(y=\sin(4x^3)\)
1
Identify the layers.
Outer: \(f(u)=\sin u\).   Inner: \(g(x)=4x^3\).
2
Differentiate the outer (the derivative of \(\sin\) is \(\cos\)), keeping the inside in place.
\(f'(g(x))=\cos(4x^3)\)
3
Differentiate the inner.
\(g'(x)=12x^2\)
4
Multiply.
\(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\cos(4x^3)\cdot 12x^2=12x^2\cos(4x^3)\)
Example 03 Differentiate \(y=e^{x^2-3x}\)
1
Identify the layers.
Outer: \(f(u)=e^u\).   Inner: \(g(x)=x^2-3x\).
2
The derivative of \(e^u\) is \(e^u\), which means it's its own derivative. Keep the inside in place.
\(f'(g(x))=e^{x^2-3x}\)
3
Differentiate the inner.
\(g'(x)=2x-3\)
4
Multiply.
\(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=e^{x^2-3x}\cdot(2x-3)=(2x-3)e^{x^2-3x}\)
Example 04 Differentiate \(y=x^2\sin(5x)\)  (Chain Rule + Product Rule)
1
Recognize this as a product: \(x^2\) times \(\sin(5x)\). Apply the Product Rule first.
\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[uv]=u'v+uv'\)
\(u=x^2,\quad v=\sin(5x)\)
2
Differentiate \(u=x^2\).
\(u'=2x\)
3
Differentiate \(v=\sin(5x)\) using the Chain Rule.
\(v'=\cos(5x)\cdot 5=5\cos(5x)\)
Outer: \(\sin\), inner: \(5x\), inner derivative: \(5\).
4
Assemble with the Product Rule formula.
\(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=2x\cdot\sin(5x)+x^2\cdot 5\cos(5x)\)
\(=2x\sin(5x)+5x^2\cos(5x)\)
Example 05 Differentiate \(y=\cos^3(2x)\)  (three layers)
1
Rewrite to see the layers clearly: \(y=[\cos(2x)]^3\).
Three layers: outermost \((\square)^3\), then \(\cos(\square)\), then \(2x\).
2
Differentiate the outermost layer (Power Rule on the whole cosine expression).
\(3[\cos(2x)]^2\)
3
Multiply by the derivative of the next layer: \(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\cos(2x)]\).
\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\cos(2x)]=-\sin(2x)\cdot 2=-2\sin(2x)\)
Chain Rule again: derivative of \(\cos\) is \(-\sin\), times the derivative of \(2x\) which is \(2\).
4
Combine all layers.
\(\dfrac{dy}{dx}=3[\cos(2x)]^2\cdot(-2\sin(2x))=-6\sin(2x)\cos^2(2x)\)

04 · Common Mistakes

What trips people up

These are the errors that show up most often on AP exams. Read each one carefully.

✗ Forgetting the inner derivative

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\sin(x^2)] = \cos(x^2)\)

Stopped after differentiating the outer. Missing the \(\cdot 2x\).

✓ Correct

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[\sin(x^2)] = \cos(x^2)\cdot 2x\)

Always multiply by \(g'(x)\). Don't stop after the outer derivative.

✗ Changing the inside

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[(x^3+1)^4]=4(3x^2)^3\)

Differentiated the inside inside the outer. The inside must stay as-is when you differentiate the outer.

✓ Correct

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[(x^3+1)^4]=4(x^3+1)^3\cdot 3x^2\)

Keep \((x^3+1)\) intact in the outer derivative. Then multiply by \(3x^2\) separately.

✗ Applying chain rule to a simple power

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[x^5]=5x^4\cdot 1\cdot\text{"chain rule"}\)

The inner function is just \(x\), whose derivative is 1. The Chain Rule still applies, it just does nothing extra here.

✓ Understand why it's trivial

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[x^5]=5x^4\cdot\underbrace{\dfrac{d}{dx}[x]}_{=1}=5x^4\)

The Chain Rule is always happening. When the inside is just \(x\), it contributes a factor of 1 and disappears.

✗ Forgetting Chain Rule with \(e^{f(x)}\)

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[e^{3x^2}]=e^{3x^2}\)

Yes, \(e^u\) differentiates to itself, but you still need \(\cdot g'(x)\).

✓ Correct

\(\dfrac{d}{dx}[e^{3x^2}]=e^{3x^2}\cdot 6x\)

The outer \(e^u\) stays as \(e^{3x^2}\), multiplied by the inner derivative \(6x\).
